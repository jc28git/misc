Problem 1 17.8 Drive-in movies

Private demand P = 20 − 0.1Q; marginal cost MC = 0.1Q; external marginal benefit EMB = 2 − 0.01Q.

  1. a.

    Set private demand equal to marginal cost:

    20 − 0.1Q = 0.1Q ⇒ Q = 100 P = 20 − 0.1(100) = $10

    The market shows 100 movies at $10.

  2. b.

    The social demand curve adds the external marginal benefit vertically to private demand:

    SD = D + EMB = (20 − 0.1Q) + (2 − 0.01Q) = 22 − 0.11Q

    EMB runs from $2 at Q = 0 to $0 at Q = 200. SD runs from $22 at Q = 0 to $0 at Q = 200.

  3. c.

    The Movie Czar sets social demand equal to marginal cost:

    22 − 0.11Q = 0.1Q ⇒ Q = 22/0.21 ≈ 105 P = 22 − 0.11(105) ≈ $10.45

    Optimal quantity ≈ 105 movies at a price of about $10.45.

  4. d.

    The deadweight loss is the triangle between SD and MC from the private output (100) to the efficient output (≈105), shaded in the graph in (b). The answer key computes:

    DWL = ½ × (105 − 100) × (10.45 − 9.50) = $2.375
    Using the hint directly: at Q = 100 the last unit's external benefit is EMB = 2 − 0.01(100) = $1, so the triangle's height is SD − MC = 11 − 10 = $1 and its base is 104.76 − 100 = 4.76. DWL = ½ × 4.76 × 1 ≈ $2.38. Same answer to rounding.
  5. e.

    Yes. Privately run theaters underproduce because they ignore the benefit to viewers outside the fence. Government can raise output to the socially optimal level. It could also reach the same result with a price-based policy, such as a per-movie subsidy to private producers equal to the EMB at the optimum.

Problem 2 17.9 Leather

Inverse demand P = 50 − 0.5Q; private MC = 0.45Q; external marginal cost EMC = 0.05Q.

  1. a.

    Social marginal cost is SMC = MC + EMC = 0.45Q + 0.05Q = 0.5Q. The efficient output sets MB = SMC:

    50 − 0.5Q = 0.5Q ⇒ Q* = 50 P = 50 − 0.5(50) = $25

    Set the quota at 50 units. The market price with the quota is $25.

  2. b.

    The tax equals the external damage at the efficient output: EMC(50) = 0.05 × 50 = $2.50 per unit. With the tax, producers' marginal cost becomes MC = 0.45Q + 2.50. Producers set MB = MC:

    50 − 0.5Q = 0.45Q + 2.50 ⇒ Q = 50 P = 50 − 0.5(50) = $25

    50 units are bought and sold. Buyers pay $25; sellers keep $25 − $2.50 = $22.50.

    For comparison, the unregulated market sets 50 − 0.5Q = 0.45Q, giving Q ≈ 52.6, so the policy trims output by about 2.6 units.

Problem 3 17.11 Genetic testing

  1. a.

    A positive externality. The person who buys the test gets private benefits: ancestry, links to relatives. Law enforcement also gets potential links to suspects, and the more people who are tested, the more matches there are. That benefit goes to law enforcement (and society), not to the buyer.

  2. b.

    Markets underprovide goods that carry positive externalities. Buyers act on their private marginal benefit, which lies below the social marginal benefit, so the market quantity falls short of the efficient quantity. There is likely too little genetic testing.

  3. c.

    The goal is more testing, which rules out taxes and cap-and-trade; both reduce quantity. A quota (requiring testing) would raise output but raises serious constitutional and privacy concerns. That leaves a subsidy, which raises the incentive to be tested without compelling anyone.

Problem 4 17.13 Sulfur dioxide

From the diagram: MBP = MAC falls from $800 to $0; MCP (external marginal cost of pollution) rises; they cross at $400.

  1. a.

    When pollution is already low, cutting one more ton is expensive: the cheap abatement options are used up. When pollution is high, the first tons can be cut cheaply. So the marginal abatement cost, read as the marginal benefit to the firm of emitting one more ton, falls as emissions rise.

  2. b.

    The efficient level of pollution is where MBP = MAC crosses MCP, at a marginal value of $400 per ton.

  3. c.

    Firms ignore MCP, so they would emit until MBP = 0. A $400 per-ton tax shifts their net marginal benefit of polluting down by $400 (the dashed line in the figure). Firms now emit until MBP = $400, which happens exactly at the efficient level, because the MCP there is $400.

  4. d.

    $600 is too high: firms emit until MBP = $600, which happens at a quantity below the efficient level (Q₁). Society gives up abatement-cost savings worth more than the damage avoided.

    $200 is too low: firms emit until MBP = $200, a quantity above the efficient level (Q₂). Those extra tons cause damage greater than their value to firms.

  1. At the point where the isoquants cross, both countries use the same labor–capital combination. Now remove a fixed amount of capital. Along the steeper isoquant (Country A), output is maintained with a smaller increase in labor than along the flatter one (Country B). Problem 8 6.17 Creekwood Lawn and Landscape

Cost-minimizing at L = 20, K = 10, Q = 10 lawns, C = $400. The $400 isocost hits L = 40 and K = 20.

  1. a.

    $400 buys at most 40 workers (horizontal intercept), so w = 400/40 = $10/hour. It buys at most 20 units of capital (vertical intercept), so r = 400/20 = $20/hour.

  2. b.

    At w = $20, $400 buys at most 20 workers. The $400 isocost rotates inward around K = 20 to the L-intercept at 20. It no longer reaches the Q = 10 isoquant, so serving 10 lawns for $400 is impossible. Creekwood's cost of serving its customers rises.

  3. c.

    The original bundle now costs 20 × $20 + 10 × $20 = $600. The $200 subsidy covers the difference, so Creekwood pays $400 out of pocket for exactly its old inputs. The new isocost (total spending $600) passes through (L = 20, K = 10) with the steeper slope −w/r = −1, so it hits K = 30 and L = 30.

  4. d.

    No. Labor is now relatively more expensive, and the subsidy arrives whether or not Creekwood spends it on labor. The steeper isocost through (20, 10) cuts the isoquant rather than being tangent to it, so Creekwood can lower costs by substituting toward capital: it moves to the point L* < 20, K* > 10 where an isocost with slope −1 is tangent to Q = 10.
